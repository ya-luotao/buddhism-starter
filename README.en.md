## Study and practice

Studying Buddhism means history, texts, and doctrine; the aim is to know what it says. Practicing means sitting every day; the aim is to change how perception works. Both are needed, and it helps to know which one you are doing at any given time. Reading without practicing tends to turn Buddhism into a philosophy that can be finished, after which suffering is unchanged. Practicing without reading leaves you without a map, and it becomes easy to take a stage for the destination, or a low stretch for failure.

Understanding does not reach the level at which suffering is produced. A person can accept the argument for non-self in full and the sense of self remains as it was, in the way that knowing the Müller-Lyer illusion is an illusion does not make the two lines look equal. Buddhism distinguished these from the start: wisdom from hearing, from reflection, and from cultivation are three levels, and right view is only the first factor of the eightfold path. What it offers is a training, not only a theory.

## Starting from Siddhartha's life

Most of the core teachings can be traced to something in the Buddha's own life, and entering through the biography makes it easier to see why the teachings are these and not others. One caution first: what the sources can confirm about his life is a skeleton. Details such as the four sights outside the palace come from later biographies. The legendary material still has value, because it shows what later generations considered most important.

He was born around the fifth century BCE, near the present border of Nepal and India, into the nobility of a small state of the Shakya clan. The biographies record that he grew up in comfort and that at twenty-nine he went outside the city, saw an old man, a sick man, a corpse, and a wandering ascetic, understood that wealth could not prevent the first three, and left home. What the sources can confirm is that he did leave his family as a young man to become a renunciant, which was not an unusual choice in the India of the time.

He trained first under Alara Kalama and then Uddaka Ramaputta, learned the deepest meditative attainments then known, and was invited by both to stay and lead their communities with them. He left, on the grounds that however deep these states went, one came out of them, and suffering remained. This is the source of the principle that meditative states are not the goal. He then turned to the other extreme, six years of austerities; the texts record that he fasted until he could feel his spine through his abdomen, and concluded that this path did not lead anywhere either. He accepted milk-rice from a village girl, and the five companions practicing with him took this as a lapse and left. This episode is the source of the Middle Way: indulgence and self-mortification had both been tried, and neither worked.

At thirty-five he sat beneath a tree, and the biographies say he resolved not to rise until he had seen through. What happened that night the texts describe in the terms of his later teaching: he saw dependent origination, how suffering arises and how it ceases. Afterward he hesitated over whether to teach, judging that the matter ran against ordinary human habit and would be hard to understand; the biographies say that he decided to teach only after the god Brahma requested it. He found the five companions who had left him and gave his first discourse at the Deer Park near Varanasi. Its content was the four noble truths and the eightfold path. This is the [Discourse on Setting the Wheel of Dhamma in Motion](https://suttacentral.net/sn56.11/en/sujato).

For the next forty-five years he traveled the Ganges plain teaching, established the monastic community, answered a great many questions, and declined to answer some, such as whether the world is finite and whether an awakened one exists after death. His reason was that these questions do not lead toward the cessation of suffering, like a man struck by a poisoned arrow who insists on knowing who shot it before allowing it to be removed. At eighty he fell ill after a meal and died in a grove near Kushinagar. Asked by his disciples whom to follow afterward, he told them to take the teaching as their teacher and themselves as their refuge, and named no successor. His last recorded words were that all conditioned things are subject to decay, and that they should strive on with diligence.

Several features of Buddhism follow from this account. It begins with one person's predicament rather than with a revelation. Its founder claimed only to have rediscovered an old road. Its method was found after two failures. It sets metaphysical questions aside. It establishes no supreme authority. In the history that followed, each of these was departed from to some degree.

## Common misconceptions

| Common claim | What the sources say |
|---|---|
| The Buddha is a god; worshipping him brings protection | "Buddha" is a title, Sanskrit *buddha*, meaning one who has awakened. Shakyamuni was a historical person who lived around the fifth century BCE. In the early texts he explicitly denies being a god or a celestial being. His state after death is one of the questions he declined to answer. |
| Incense, vows, and offerings are the core of Buddhism | This is the merit-making level of lay practice. It existed in the Buddha's time but was never the goal. The transactional pattern of making a vow and repaying it is a pattern of Chinese folk religion applied to Buddhist figures. |
| Buddhism is pessimistic and rejects life | The Buddha's first discourse rules out two extremes: indulgence in pleasure and self-mortification. What he opposed was craving and clinging, not desire as such, and not life. |
| "Everything is empty" means nothing exists | Emptiness means the absence of independent existence: nothing, including the self, exists apart from its conditions. It denies independent entities, not phenomena. The [*Mulamadhyamakakarika*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C5%ABlamadhyamakak%C4%81rik%C4%81) explicitly rejects reading emptiness as nihilism. |
| Awakening is a permanent state of bliss | What the traditions call awakening is a change in how perception works: the self-model goes from transparent to visible and is recognized as a model. Ordinary functioning continues; it is not a sustained special state. It occurs in stages, and after the first shift suffering remains; what decreases is the resistance to it. |
| Meditation means emptying the mind | Buddhist insight practice means seeing clearly. Noticing that attention has wandered and returning to the object is itself the practice. Aiming at a blank mind is traditionally regarded as an error. |
| Buddhism is Zen, and Zen is self-help | Zen is one school of East Asian Buddhism, built on several centuries of early Buddhism and Madhyamaka. Read without that foundation, the Zen records tend to yield only style. |
| Buddhism is science, or anticipated quantum physics | Neither. Buddhism is a phenomenology of suffering and the self together with a training. Its description of the world uses the assumptions of ancient India. Presenting it as science is a modernist narrative of the twentieth century onward. |
| Rebirth is a Buddhist discovery | Rebirth was the general view of India in the Buddha's time; Buddhism inherited and reworked it. It is load-bearing in the early texts, but a beginner can set it aside at first. |
| Buddhists must be vegetarian | The early monastic community ate what was offered. Full vegetarianism among Chinese monastics dates from a sixth-century imperial initiative. Theravada and Tibetan monastics are not required to be vegetarian, and lay people never were. |
| One must take refuge, go to a temple, find a master | Taking refuge is a formal statement of direction, not a membership. Neither reading nor practice requires it. A teacher and a community matter at a certain stage, to keep the practice from going wrong, not as a credential. |
| Buddhism rejects thinking and demands belief | In the early texts the Buddha asks listeners to test things for themselves; the [*Kalama Sutta*](https://suttacentral.net/an3.65/en/sujato) is the most frequently cited passage. Faith is listed first among the five faculties, but it is the trust any training requires: doing what the instructions say before one can verify them. |

## Core teachings

The Buddha compared himself to a physician, and the four noble truths follow the form of a diagnosis: the illness, its cause, the possibility of cure, and the prescription.

**Suffering.** Pali *dukkha*. "Suffering" is a heavy translation; the sense is closer to unsatisfactoriness, instability, unreliability. Three kinds: evident pain; the loss that comes with change; and the subtlest, the inherent instability of anything constructed. Buddhism does not hold that life is entirely painful. It holds that anything maintained by grasping is ultimately unreliable.

**Origin.** The cause of suffering is craving and clinging. Not desire, but the grip that insists it must be so, it must be mine. The most central object of that grip is the self.

**Cessation.** Grasping can cease, and when it does, suffering ceases. This state is called nirvana, literally "extinguishing"; what is extinguished is greed, hatred, and delusion. It is not death, not heaven, and not annihilation. Asked whether an awakened one exists after death, the Buddha declined to answer and held that the question was ill-formed.

**Path.** The prescription is the eightfold path, in three groups: wisdom (right view, right intention), ethics (right speech, right action, right livelihood), and concentration (right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration). It is a training. Right view comes first, but it is only the first step.

The three marks. Impermanence: everything conditioned is changing. Non-self: nothing has an independent, permanent core, the self included. The third is given as suffering in the Theravada and as the peace of nirvana in the Chinese tradition.

Dependent origination. When this exists, that exists; when this arises, that arises. Everything is the product of conditions; nothing exists outside of conditions. This is the basis of non-self and emptiness.

The five aggregates. Buddhism analyzes a person into five groups: form (the body), feeling (the pleasant or unpleasant tone), perception (recognition), formations (volition and habit), and consciousness. Examined one by one, each is changing, none is "I," and no owner is found outside the five. Non-self is the conclusion of this analysis; practice is carrying the analysis out repeatedly in direct experience. The source is the [Discourse on the Characteristic of Non-Self](https://suttacentral.net/sn22.59/en/sujato).

The two arrows. The first arrow is pain. The second is the resistance to pain, the "why me," the wish to escape. Practitioners are struck by the first arrow as well; what practice removes is the second. This is a fairly accurate summary of what Buddhism actually promises. The source is the [*Sallatha Sutta*](https://suttacentral.net/sn36.6/en/sujato), SN 36.6.

Skillful means. The teaching is adjusted to the capacity of the listener; the same matter is put differently to different people. This explains why teachings vary so widely within Buddhism, and also the origin of devotional Buddhism: the lower rungs were meant to lead upward, and the rungs later became the whole.

## How devotional Buddhism formed

The devotional level existed from the beginning. Later history destroyed the training level beside it, leaving the devotional level standing alone. This section follows the Chinese case, which is the one the author knows.

Monastic and lay practice were two different things from the start. Monastics practiced the path of release, aiming at nirvana. Lay followers gave, kept the precepts, and accumulated merit, aiming at a good life now and a better rebirth. The Buddha himself taught lay people about wealth, family, and rebirth in good destinations. He designed this level; it was simply never treated as the destination. Lay followers were always the great majority.

His death left a vacuum. The relics were distributed, stupas were built, and circumambulation and offerings became the main lay practice. Images of the Buddha did not appear until around the first century CE; before that he was represented by a footprint, a wheel, or a tree, on the understanding that he was no longer anywhere.

The Mahayana wrote petition into scripture. The Universal Gate chapter of the *Lotus Sutra* says that calling the name of Avalokiteshvara saves one from fire and flood; in the Pure Land sutras Amitabha vows that those who recite his name will be reborn in his land. Behind this lies the deliberate design of skillful means: the teaching is adjusted to capacity, and the lower rungs also lead upward. The risk lies in the same place: the rungs may become the whole.

Entering China, Buddhism was fitted into another pattern. China already had ancestor rites, a bureaucratic underworld, and a transactional model of petitioning gods. Buddhism merged with these: the Ghost Festival came to serve the ancestors, Kshitigarbha became lord of the underworld, the Ten Kings of Hell are a native Chinese development, Avalokiteshvara was gradually feminized from the Song dynasty on and absorbed local goddesses, and the image of Maitreya was replaced by the Budai monk of the tenth century. Incense itself is an Indian form of offering, but burning paper money, making and repaying vows, and treating buddhas and bodhisattvas as officials who can be approached with gifts is the framework of Chinese folk religion.

The doctrinal lineages were once physically destroyed. In the persecution of 845 monasteries were dismantled and libraries scattered, and the schools that depended on large textual corpora, Yogacara, Huayan, and Tiantai, declined from then on. What survived were the two schools requiring almost no infrastructure: Chan, which did not depend on scripture, and Pure Land, which needed one name. After the Song the two merged; from the Ming and Qing on, the economic basis of monasteries shifted to paid rituals for the dead. In the 1920s the reformer Taixu criticized the Buddhism of his day for its preoccupation with funerals and spirits and proposed a "Buddhism for the human world"; the large Taiwanese organizations of today descend from that line.

The twentieth century brought another break. After 1949 most monasteries in mainland China closed and monastic education stopped; when religious activity resumed in the 1980s, buildings and incense were rebuilt first, and the teacher-to-student transmission of meditation training recovered most slowly.

What most people see today, then, is the lay level plus the folk-religion pattern plus tourism, while the training level that once stood beside them suffered the greatest loss. This is not unique to Buddhism; the movement from the Sermon on the Mount to indulgences belongs to the same class of process. Most believers need help with ordinary life, and the supply adjusts accordingly.

## A map of the traditions

Three main branches:

| Branch | Region | Canonical language | Character | Practice methods |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Theravada | Sri Lanka, Thailand, Myanmar, Cambodia, Laos | Pali | Closest to early Buddhism; doctrinally conservative; the clearest stage maps | Concentration and insight (Mahasi, Goenka, Thai Forest, and others) |
| Mahayana (East Asian) | China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam | Chinese, translated from Sanskrit | The bodhisattva path; emptiness and buddha-nature; many schools | Chan/Zen (sitting, koans), Pure Land recitation, calming and insight |
| Vajrayana (Tibetan) | Tibet, Mongolia, Bhutan, Nepal | Tibetan | Mahayana plus tantra; strong emphasis on the guru and lineage | Preliminaries, deity practice, Mahamudra, Dzogchen |

Main schools of Chinese Buddhism:

| School | In one line | Representative texts |
|---|---|---|
| Chan (Zen) | Direct pointing at the mind, not relying on words; sitting or koan practice | *Platform Sutra*, *Record of Linji*, *Gateless Gate*, *Blue Cliff Record* |
| Pure Land | Recite Amitabha's name, be reborn in his land, and practice there | *Amitabha Sutra*, *Infinite Life Sutra*, *Contemplation Sutra* |
| Tiantai | A classification of the teachings together with calming-and-insight practice | *Lotus Sutra*, *Great Calming and Contemplation* |
| Huayan | Interpenetration of all phenomena; the most comprehensive metaphysics | *Avatamsaka Sutra* |
| Yogacara (Consciousness-only) | Everything is consciousness; eight consciousnesses, three natures; the most detailed psychology | *Thirty Verses*, *Treatise on Consciousness-only*, *Samdhinirmocana Sutra* |
| Sanlun (Madhyamaka) | Emptiness | *Middle Treatise*, *Hundred Treatise*, *Twelve Gate Treatise* |
| Vinaya | Study and practice of the monastic code | *Four-Part Vinaya* |

This guide leans toward early Buddhist doctrine and the practice methods of the Theravada and Zen. It says little about Tibetan Buddhism and Pure Land, because the author does not know them well, and because the entry requirements of the Tibetan path (guru, lineage, empowerment) are an additional layer for the readers this guide is written for.

## Reading path

The recommendation is to begin with early Buddhism rather than Zen. Zen texts are short and concentrated, but without early Buddhism underneath they tend to yield only style. Everything later is commentary on the early layer. The order is as follows; one book at each step is enough.

**Step one, an overview.**

- Walpola Rahula, [*What the Buddha Taught*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_the_Buddha_Taught). A Theravada perspective, a little over a hundred pages.
- [Rupert Gethin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupert_Gethin), *The Foundations of Buddhism*. An academic treatment covering all three branches.
- In Chinese: Yinshun, [*An Outline of the Buddha Dharma*](https://cbetaonline.dila.edu.tw/zh/Y0008) (佛法概论), one of the most systematic introductions in the language, historical-critical in method.

**Step two, the early texts.** This layer is the closest to psychology and contains the least metaphysics.

- [Bhikkhu Bodhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhikkhu_Bodhi), ed., *In the Buddha's Words*. An anthology from the Pali canon arranged by theme, with an introduction to each chapter.
- The [*Dhammapada*](https://suttacentral.net/dhp). Short.
- The originals can be read on [SuttaCentral](https://suttacentral.net/), where Bhikkhu Sujato's translations are free. Suggested first reading: [Setting the Wheel in Motion](https://suttacentral.net/sn56.11/en/sujato) (SN 56.11), [The Characteristic of Non-Self](https://suttacentral.net/sn22.59/en/sujato) (SN 22.59), [The Foundations of Mindfulness](https://suttacentral.net/mn10/en/sujato) (MN 10), [Mindfulness of Breathing](https://suttacentral.net/mn118/en/sujato) (MN 118), the [*Kalama Sutta*](https://suttacentral.net/an3.65/en/sujato) (AN 3.65), and [The Arrow](https://suttacentral.net/sn36.6/en/sujato) (SN 36.6).

**Step three, Madhyamaka.** The precise meaning of emptiness.

- Nagarjuna, [*Mulamadhyamakakarika*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C5%ABlamadhyamakak%C4%81rik%C4%81). Jay Garfield's *The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way* is a verse-by-verse commentary by an analytic philosopher, suited to readers accustomed to argument. Siderits and Katsura's *Nagarjuna's Middle Way* is the stricter translation. The Wikipedia entry lists the English translations.

**Step four, Zen.** With the earlier foundation in place, it becomes possible to see that Zen expresses the same content in a different grammar.

- The [*Platform Sutra*](https://cbetaonline.dila.edu.tw/zh/T2008) of the Sixth Patriarch. Short; several English translations exist.
- The [*Record of Linji*](https://cbetaonline.dila.edu.tw/zh/T1985) and the [*Gateless Gate*](https://cbetaonline.dila.edu.tw/zh/T2005).
- Shunryu Suzuki, [*Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zen_Mind,_Beginner%27s_Mind). On the attitude of practice.

**Step five, modern criticism and parallels.**

- Evan Thompson, [*Why I Am Not a Buddhist*](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300226553/why-i-am-not-a-buddhist/). A critique of the "Buddhism is science" narrative by a scholar of Buddhist philosophy and cognitive science; the criticism comes from inside.
- History: Gethin above covers it; for more depth, Akira Hirakawa, *A History of Indian Buddhism*.
- Work in Western philosophy and cognitive science that runs parallel to the argument for non-self: Derek Parfit, [*Reasons and Persons*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reasons_and_Persons), Part Three; Thomas Metzinger, [*The Ego Tunnel*](https://www.basicbooks.com/titles/thomas-metzinger/the-ego-tunnel/9780465020690/); Anil Seth, [*Being You*](https://www.anilseth.com/being-you/). From personal identity, the self-model, and predictive processing respectively, they reach conclusions close to the analysis of the five aggregates.
- If the thread of "mind constructs the world" is of interest, Yogacara is the place to go: Vasubandhu's *Thirty Verses* with a commentary.

**Not recommended as a starting point.** Tibetan tantra and guru circles: the entry requirements are high, and self-appointed teachers charging for instruction are common. Popular self-help books with "Zen" in the title. Any system that promises rapid awakening or sells instruction by the course.

## Practice

Mindfulness of breathing, *anapanasati*, is the starting point the Buddha recommended, and it is used in every tradition. The minimal form is as follows.

**Posture.** A chair or a cushion; the spine straight but not rigid, not leaning against a backrest, hands in the lap, eyes closed or lowered. If a knee hurts, adjust the posture rather than endure it.

**Object.** Place attention on the sensation of breathing, either the air at the nostrils or the movement of the abdomen. Choose one and keep it. Do not control the breath.

**Wandering.** Wandering is the normal condition. When you notice it, return to the breath without judgment. The act of returning is the practice itself; practice is not sustained concentration but repeated return. However many times attention wanders, it returns as many times, and the sitting has not failed.

**Duration.** Twenty to thirty minutes a day, at a fixed time, with a timer. Twenty minutes daily is better than two hours at the weekend. For the first few months, do only this.

**States.** Calm, joy, light, the fading of bodily sensation: these will appear sooner or later. Observe them when they do, without pursuing or grasping. Pursuing states is the error every tradition warns about.

**After a few weeks.** Noting can be added, that is, silently naming what drew attention away: thinking, hearing, pain. Or a body scan. This is the time to read a practice manual. [Bhante Gunaratana's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henepola_Gunaratana) *Mindfulness in Plain English* is a commonly recommended introduction; Thanissaro Bhikkhu's [*With Each and Every Breath*](https://www.dhammatalks.org/books/WithEachAndEveryBreath/Section0001.html) is free; [Mahasi Sayadaw's](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/mahasi/) writings are the source of the noting method, and his [*The Progress of Insight*](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/mahasi/progress.html) is the source of the stage map; [Culadasa's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culadasa) *The Mind Illuminated* is the most systematically structured concentration manual, in ten stages. The author was removed from his teaching organization in 2019 over personal conduct; the book can be assessed on its own.

**Retreats.** After a few months of daily practice, a first intensive retreat can be considered. The most accessible is the ten-day course in the tradition of [S.N. Goenka](https://www.dhamma.org/), free and non-sectarian, with centers in many countries. The [Insight Meditation Society](https://www.dharma.org/) and similar centers run retreats of varying length. Monasteries in Thailand, Myanmar, and Sri Lanka accept foreign practitioners. Entering a ten-day course without a daily foundation carries a risk of adverse reactions for some people.

**A teacher.** At some stage a teacher is needed, because there are places on the path that are hard to judge alone: mistaking the joy of a particular stage for awakening, and the low stretch that follows. Choose a teacher by everyday conduct, not by claims. Money, sex, secrecy, claimed attainments, or discouragement of questions: if any of these appears, leave.

**Adverse effects.** They exist. Prolonged intensive practice can bring on anxiety, depersonalization, worsened insomnia, and the resurfacing of traumatic memory. If this happens, stop, seek an experienced teacher, and see a clinician if needed. Willoughby Britton's group at Brown University studies this problem, and [Cheetah House](https://www.cheetahhouse.org/) is the support resource they established.

## Common errors

- Emptiness sickness, or spiritual bypassing: using "no self" or "all is empty" to avoid what needs to be dealt with. Emptiness denies independent entities, not responsibility.
- Pursuing states: making a particular calm or joy the goal.
- Treating the map as a score: stage maps are useful; using them to grade oneself is harmful.
- Misidentifying the arising-and-passing stage: at a certain stage light, joy, clarity, and confidence appear; the tradition lists ten "corruptions of insight" and warns that this is where the stage is most often mistaken for awakening. What usually follows is a low stretch, which is also part of the path.
- Turning "the present" into a new identity: the observer, presence, awareness. As soon as any of these becomes something to settle into, the self has returned under a new name.
- Reading without practice, or practice without reading: the first yields a philosophy, the second no map.
- Dependence on a guru: the point of lineage is to keep the method intact, not to surrender judgment.
- Using Buddhism for problems it is not suited to: depression, anxiety disorders, chronic pain, trauma. See a clinician first. Mindfulness-based therapies have clinical evidence, but they are techniques extracted from Buddhism with the original framework removed, and they have indications.
- Treating Buddhism as science: it is a phenomenology of suffering and the self together with a training; its cosmology is an assumption.
- Treating practice as project management: progress in practice has no milestones that can be set in advance.

## FAQ

**Is belief in rebirth required?** Not at the outset. The early texts assume rebirth and the motivation for ending suffering rests on it. It can be set aside, with the awareness that one is reading texts whose motivational frame differs from one's own. The practice itself does not depend on it.

**Is vegetarianism required?** Not for lay people. The early monastic community ate what was offered; meat was permitted if the monk had not seen, heard, or suspected that the animal was killed for him. Full vegetarianism among Chinese monastics dates from the sixth century onward.

**Is taking refuge required?** Taking refuge is a formal statement that the Buddha, the teaching, and the community are one's direction. It is not a membership. Neither reading nor practice depends on it, and the decision can be made later.

**Is going to a temple required?** No. A place is needed to find a teacher and a community; it may be a temple, a meditation center, or a private group.

**Can an atheist do this?** Yes. Buddhism assumes no creator, and the gods in its cosmology are themselves beings within the cycle of rebirth, irrelevant to liberation.

**What is the relation to psychotherapy?** Complementary, not a replacement. Mindfulness-based stress reduction and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy are techniques extracted from Buddhism with clinical evidence. A diagnosed condition should be treated first; practice once stable.

**What is the relation to *The Power of Now* and similar books?** [Tolle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Power_of_Now) presents a popular version of presence teaching with Advaita and Zen coloring, and the direction is broadly the same. "Watching the thinker" corresponds to the self-model becoming visible; the "pain-body" corresponds to the second arrow. What such books lack is a method, a stage map, warnings about errors, and a community; they describe the target state and little else.

**What is the relation to Daoism and Confucianism?** The three interpenetrated in China for over a thousand years. The language of Zen was strongly influenced by Daoism, and Song-Ming Neo-Confucianism in turn absorbed Buddhist theories of mind. Terms such as "naturalness" and "non-action" in the Zen records carry the merged usage.

**How long until there are results?** Changes in attention can be noticed within weeks. Deeper changes take years, and there is no schedule.

**What is the difference between Buddhism, Buddhist studies, and practice?** Buddhism is a religion and an institution; Buddhist studies is the study of it; practice is the training. This guide touches all three, with the weight on the last two.

## Glossary

- Buddha. One who has awakened; a title.
- Dharma (Pali *dhamma*). The Buddha's teaching; also, any phenomenon.
- Sangha. The community of practitioners; in the narrow sense, the monastics.
- Dukkha. Unsatisfactoriness, instability, unreliability; conventionally "suffering."
- Impermanence (*anicca*). Everything conditioned is changing.
- Non-self (*anatta*). Nothing has an independent, permanent core, the self included.
- Emptiness (*shunyata*). The absence of independent existence; nothing exists apart from conditions. Not nothingness.
- Dependent origination. When this exists, that exists; everything is the product of conditions.
- Nirvana. The extinguishing of greed, hatred, and delusion. Not death, not heaven, not annihilation.
- Arahant. In the Theravada, one who has completed the path.
- Bodhisattva. In the Mahayana, one who aims at buddhahood while benefiting others.
- Samatha. The practice of steadying and concentrating the mind.
- Vipassana. The practice of seeing phenomena clearly; "insight meditation" is its systematized form.
- Jhana. Deep states of concentration in samatha practice, in several levels.
- Mindfulness (*sati*). Remembering to keep attention on what is happening now. Modern usage has broadened considerably.
- Karma. Action, especially intentional action, and its consequences. Not fate.
- Samsara. The cycle of birth and death; an assumption of early Buddhism.
- Skillful means (*upaya*). Adjusting the teaching to the listener.
- The three trainings. Ethics, concentration, wisdom; the three groups of the eightfold path.
- The three refuges. Buddha, Dharma, Sangha.
- The five precepts. Not killing, not stealing, no sexual misconduct, not lying, no intoxicants.
- Koan. A question or story used in Zen for contemplation.

## About this guide

This guide leans toward the historical-critical approach that gives priority to early Buddhism, and toward Theravada and Zen practice methods; it says little about Tibetan Buddhism and Pure Land. The author is also a beginner, and errors are likely. Corrections and better recommendations are welcome as issues or pull requests. The guide originated in a conversation in September 2026 that began with death, passed through the self, reality, suffering, and awakening, and ended at Buddhism.
